First, the question you asked

Does the Maestro run things in parallel or serial?

Both, and the distinction is the whole reason we built what we built.

Motion is parallel. Every channel has its own speed and acceleration limit, and the Maestro's firmware updates all of them every servo period — 20 ms by default. Twelve panels can be mid-travel at once, each at its own rate, and nothing you do to one affects another. That part is genuinely simultaneous and it is what makes a Maestro feel good.

Sequences are serial. The script engine is a single stack machine running one instruction at a time. There is exactly one script, and restartScript(n) does what it says — it restarts, abandoning whatever was running and jumping to subroutine n. You cannot have a holoprojector idling while the pies do something else, because the second command kills the first. A "wait" in a sequence is a delay that stops the whole script.

That is the ceiling we went past. MaestroPCA runs four sequences at once on disjoint channels, plus a background layer that gets displaced by a real gesture and resumes by itself when the channels free up. The idle holo keeps sweeping while a panel opens; the sweep comes back on its own when the panel is done. No Maestro can do that, at any price.

Worth being precise about what "isolation" buys you, because it is often confused with parallelism. A Maestro's real selling point is that it is a different CPU: your host sketch can stall on Usb.Task() or a delay(750) and the panels keep easing. That is isolation, not concurrency — and our co-processor has exactly the same property, for £4.

The 4.88 µs number, since it is the one place the PCA route is genuinely behind. A PCA9685 divides its period into 4096 steps, so at 50 Hz each step is 20000 ÷ 4096 = 4.88 µs. Our targets are quarter-µs and get rounded to the nearest step. On a 1000 µs throw that is 0.5% — about half a degree on a 90° servo, which is comfortably inside the slop of a printed linkage and a 3D-printed horn. It has never been visible on your bench. But it is why the oscillator trim in Setup matters: the board's RC clock is only nominally 25 MHz, and an untrimmed one shifts every pulse by more than that rounding does.

Is an ESP32 worth building?

Short answer: only for the radio — and that might be reason enough.

It would not improve the motion. That is the thing to be clear about before spending a weekend on it. The Nano already runs the whole engine — four tracks, easing, background layers, release-when-parked — at 100 Hz in 27 KB of flash and 1.2 KB of RAM, and it is not close to struggling. Motion quality on this route is set by the PCA9685's 4.88 µs steps, not by the processor asking for them. A 240 MHz dual-core would make exactly the same servos do exactly the same thing.

What it would actually add is wireless. An ESP32 can hold the sequences and serve the editor. That means: change a routine at a convention without opening the dome; trigger a slot from your phone; watch the serial monitor from across the room; drop a new sequences.h in over the air instead of unplugging a Nano. For a droid that gets taken places, that is a real quality-of-life difference rather than a spec-sheet one.

And it costs the same. An ESP32 dev board is about £5, the same as a Nano. So the trade is not money — it is 3.3 V logic, a fussier toolchain, and brown-out sensitivity, against WiFi and effectively unlimited sequence storage.

One genuinely interesting option we have not considered: for a dome of 16 servos or fewer, an ESP32 can drive them directly. It has 16 hardware LEDC channels (8 high-speed, 8 low-speed, across 4 timers), and at 50 Hz it can use 16-bit resolution — about 0.3 µs a step, better than the PCA9685 and close to the Maestro. No I²C, no expander, no address jumpers. The moment you need a seventeenth channel you are back to a PCA9685, so it does not suit the MK4 dome plus body — but for a standalone prop or the body alone it is the tidiest thing on this page.

What about a Teensy?

It solves a problem you do not have.

A Teensy 4.1 is a 600 MHz Cortex-M7 with superb timing, ~27 PWM-capable pins and 8 MB of flash. It would drive servos directly with sub-microsecond resolution — the best motion on this page, on paper.

Two things spoil it for this job. The stock Servo library only handles 12 motors on a Teensy 4.x, so past a dozen channels you are writing timer code yourself or adding a PCA9685 anyway — at which point the 600 MHz is doing nothing the Nano was not. And it is £28 against £4, with no radio.

Where a Teensy earns its money is hard-real-time work: reading encoders, closed-loop control, audio DSP. A droid panel that has to move 30° over 400 ms is none of those. If you ever build a walking or balancing rig, revisit it.

Since this page was written, two of its "not built" columns stopped being hypothetical. MpcaLedcOutput drives servos off ESP32 pins, examples/Esp32Droid is the co-processor with a web page, and MpcaSplitOutput puts a second board on the far end of a wire. All of it runs against the same sequences.h and the same editor; none of it has met real silicon yet.

ESP32 + the PCA9685 boards, instead of a Nano

So the honest summary is: you are buying a radio and some flash for £1. Not better motion — the servos cannot tell which chip asked. Whether that is worth changing a working board for comes down to whether "fire a routine from my phone without opening the dome" is a thing you will actually do at an event. If it is, this is the cheapest way to get it, and nothing about your sequences, your endpoints or your host sketch changes.
One thing to know before ordering. If you ever want the ESP32's own pins driving servos — 0.3 µs steps, no expander at all — that path tops out at 16 channels, because that is how many LEDC channels the silicon has. Your dome and body together are past that, so on your droid the PCA9685s stay and the ESP32 is a host with a radio. On a smaller rig it would be a genuinely tidier machine.

Two ESP32s, one master — getting past sixteen channels

Since the ESP32's own pins top out at sixteen, the obvious next thought is a second board. It works, and MpcaSplitOutput does it: boards 0..n-1 go to a local output, everything above goes down a serial link to an Esp32Slave that does nothing but make pulses. The channel table needs no change at all — it already says which board each channel is on, and nothing ever promised a "board" was a PCA9685.

But do not do it for the channel count. Look at what the second ESP32 is actually doing: receiving positions and making pulses. That is precisely a PCA9685's job — and a PCA9685 does it in silicon for £5, with no firmware to flash, no second binary to keep in step with the first, and an addressing scheme that already works. Two ESP32s cost the same and give you a worse expander with a link protocol to debug.

The sixteen servo signal wires still have to physically reach the second board either way, so the wiring does not get simpler; you save two wires on the run between boards. And the resolution you gain — 0.3 µs against 4.88 µs — is half a percent of a throw, inside the slop of a printed linkage.

Where it does win: distance

I²C was never designed for long runs. It is a short, capacitance-sensitive bus, and a droid has a slip ring in the middle of it. A UART tolerates a long noisy run far better. If the body bank ends up at the far end of the loom and I²C starts misbehaving, this is the right fix — and an ESP32 pair can do it over ESP-NOW with no data wires between dome and body at all, which no PCA9685 offers at any price.

It cost 4 bytes to have. The linker drops the whole split backend when nothing references it — measured on the real AVR build, 27,010 → 27,014. So it sits in the library unused until the day I²C gives trouble, and costs nothing until then.

What if the ESP32 runs the whole 2026 droid?

Different question from the one above, and a better one. Not an ESP32 as a servo co-processor next to a Mega ADK — an ESP32 instead of the Mega ADK, running the mod2026 sketch itself.

The short version: it does not make the sketch better, it makes the CONTROLLER CHAIN better — and only if you change the controller. Everything else about mod2026 ports across without drama; the input layer is the whole job, and it is also the whole prize.

What you delete

mod2026 talks to an Xbox 360 wireless receiver through a USB Host Shield (the MAX3421E the Mega ADK has built in). That is two components and a library — XBOXRECV from USB Host Shield 2.0 — existing purely to get a controller's buttons into the sketch.

An ESP32 has Bluetooth on the die. With Bluepad32, an Xbox One/Series pad (models 1708 / 1914) or a PS4/PS5 pad connects straight to the board. No dongle, no host shield, no MAX3421E. Two boards and a USB stack become one library call.

The catch, and it is a real one: the Xbox 360 wireless controller is not Bluetooth. It uses Microsoft's own 2.4 GHz protocol, which is exactly why it needs that receiver. It cannot pair with an ESP32 at all. So this only pays off if you move to a newer pad. Keep the 360 controller and you must bolt a MAX3421E module onto the ESP32's SPI — which works, but you have then rebuilt the Mega ADK badly.
And it must be a CLASSIC ESP32. Bluepad32 needs Bluetooth BR/EDR for PS3/PS4/PS5 and the older Xbox pads, and BR/EDR does not exist on the ESP32-S3, C3, C6 or H2 — those are BLE-only. The £5 original is the right chip here, which is convenient.

Worth doing?

For a new build, yes, and it is tempting. One £5 board replaces a Mega ADK, a USB host shield and a receiver dongle; you gain WiFi for firing routines from a phone, far more storage, and better LED timing. The droid gets simpler, not more complicated.

For a droid that already works, no. Mike's runs a Mega ADK and an Xbox 360 pad, and this trade asks him to replace the controller, re-do the input layer, add level shifters and re-test the whole drive chain — to arrive at the same droid with a nicer radio. The PCA9685 co-processor gets the sequencer benefits already, on the hardware that is in the dome.

The useful middle: an ESP32 as the servo co-processor today (examples/Esp32Droid, done), which needs no change to the host at all — the Mega ADK still sends restartScript(n) and does not know what is on the other end. If the Bluetooth controller ever appeals, that is a separate change on a separate board, and neither one blocks the other.

What I would do

  • Stay on the PCA9685 route for the droid. It is the only option here that does concurrency, background idles and release-when-parked, it costs a quarter of a Maestro, and the firmware is ours — when the bench said overshoot needed headroom, we changed the engine. Nothing on this page beats that for this job.
  • Keep the Maestro option working, because other people have them and the sim exports for both. It is a fine board; it is just a 2010 answer to the question.
  • Build the ESP32 version when you want the radio, not before. With your two PCA9685s it is a £1 swap for a Nano and one #define; the motion is identical because the expander sets it, not the CPU. It is the same C++; only the PWM writes and the link change. Realistically a day, most of it on the WiFi side rather than the motion.
  • Skip the Teensy unless a future project needs real-time control rather than choreography.
  • Do not use two ESP32s to get past sixteen channels — a second PCA9685 is cheaper, needs no firmware and cannot go out of sync. Keep MpcaSplitOutput in your pocket for the day I²C struggles across the neck, which is the one thing it genuinely beats.
  • Do not re-host the 2026 droid on an ESP32 — not this droid. The prize there is deleting the USB host shield and the receiver, and collecting it means changing the controller. Worth it on a build that has not been wired yet; not worth it on one that works.
One thing worth doing regardless. The engine is already written against an abstraction — it hands positions to a list of PCA9685 objects. Making that a small interface rather than a concrete type is an hour's work, and it is what would let an ESP32 (or a Teensy driving pins directly) drop in underneath the same sequences, the same editor and the same sequences.h. Worth doing the next time that file is open, whether or not you ever buy either board.
