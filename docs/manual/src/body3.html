11 · Bricks

The sequencer desk is where a routine gets made. A brick is one part moving: it opens, it holds, it closes. You drag bricks onto a timeline, set how long each runs, and the desk compiles the lot into the frames your board actually plays.

{{CLIP:bricks|A whole figure in three clicks — pick a set of parts, pick a ready-made shape, press play. The droid performs it on the right while the timeline scrubs.}}

Making one

  1. ＋ New sequence, and name it.
  2. Drag a part from the PARTS tray onto the timeline — or use Ready-made: pick a set (all pies, all side panels) and a shape, and the whole figure lands at once as a single undoable gesture.
  3. Drag a brick's edges to stretch it. Click one to open its inspector: how long it runs, and its motion — open, open-and-close, or close.
  4. Select several bricks and set Runs for or Motion on all of them together.
  5. ▶ PLAY previews it on the model.

Things worth knowing

  • The snap picker lives in the timeline ruler's corner, next to the timing line it governs.
  • Each part has its own colour, and "Colour the model to match" paints the droid the same way, so you can see at a glance which brick is which panel.
  • You can choreograph a panel you have not wired yet. Drag in a part with no channel and it stays grey: it keeps its place and its timing, it moves the model in preview, and it is left out of the compiled frames with a named warning. The moment you give it a channel it starts driving the real servo. Nothing is lost in the meantime.
  • Advanced reveals two other ways of looking underneath a routine: Pose (set the droid channel by channel, live) and Frames (the frame list the bricks compile to). Both are useful; neither is a beginner's first move. Unticking Advanced while you are standing in one of them returns you to the bricks.
  • Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y undo and redo, and a whole ready-made figure is one step.

12 · Snap to music

♪ LOAD MUSIC takes an audio file and puts its beats on the timeline. Set the snap to the beat and every brick edge you drag lands on it, so a routine can be choreographed to a track rather than to a stopwatch.

It stays on your machine

The track never leaves the browser. Nothing is uploaded anywhere — there is nowhere for it to go, because the whole app is a file on your disk.

13 · Onto the board

A routine in your library is not yet a routine your droid can run. The board holds eight slots, 0–7, and the sketch only ever calls restartScript(0) … restartScript(7). That is the whole interface between the droid's Arduino and its servo controller.

  1. Build the routines you want in the sequencer.
  2. Open BOARD ▸ Build your sequences.h… — the builder is the only thing that changes what is on the board, and in what order.
  3. Choose which library routines land on slots 0–7. A routine past 7, or one not loaded, is simply unreachable from the controller — the list says so.
  4. Export. On a Maestro build you get a .mstr; on a PCA9685 build you get sequences.h for the MaestroPCA library.
{{CLIP:slots|The builder: your library on the left, the eight slots in order in the middle, and the validation on the right — errors, warnings, script bytes, subroutines used, and the slowest throw in the whole loadout.}}
Saving is not the same as loading

Save keeps a sequence in your library. It does not change the Maestro script. Only Build your sequences.h does that. A faded chip in the library is one that is not loaded.

Rename freely — spaces become underscores in the generated sub name.

14 · Driving real servos from the desk

With hardware connected, the amber ⚡ arm switch beside the transport sends the sequencer's output to the actual board. Play a routine and the droid performs it in front of you.

It drives through the engine, never straight at the wire — so your measured end stops, your speeds and your accelerations all apply exactly as they will when the routine is running off the board on its own.

The arm disarms itself when you leave

Leaving the sequencer drops the arm, and leaving the servo bench disconnects the board. Both are deliberate: an arm whose amber button is off screen is an arm you have forgotten. The link stays open and the servos hold where they are.

15 · Which sketch, and when

On a PCA9685 build there are two, and they are used at different times:

SketchWhereWhen
PCA_Bridgepca-studio/PCA_Bridge/PCA_Bridge.ino The bench sketch. Flash it while you are measuring. It does nothing but take commands from this simulator over USB — it turns the Channels page into the droid's control surface.
MaestroReplacementarduino/MaestroPCA/examples/MaestroReplacement/ The droid sketch. Flash it when you are done, with your measured travel baked into sequences.h. From then on it answers restartScript(n) exactly as a Maestro does, and Padawan360 cannot tell the difference.

The boards find themselves

Every sketch scans the I2C bus for PCA9685s rather than assuming they sit at 0x40 and 0x41. It sweeps the real jumper range (0x40–0x7F — six address jumpers) and numbers what it finds in ascending address order, so 0x40 + 0x42 behaves exactly as 0x40 + 0x41 would. Every sketch prints the mapping it settled on; read that line, it is the ground truth for your bus.

Why you must never trust a hand-rolled bus scan

A PCA9685 answers 0x70 out of the box. That is the All Call address, and it is enabled by default — so one chip acknowledges at its own address and at 0x70. A naive sweep turns one board into two, and the phantom board is every board at once: a write meant for "board 1" moves every servo on the droid. 0x71–0x73 are the subaddresses and are excluded for the same reason. The shared scan header exists so this is got right once rather than four times.

The trade-off, which every sketch prints: if a board drops off the bus, everything above it renumbers.

Eight boards, 128 channels

The live-drive wire protocol carries channels 0–125 for servos, with 126 the oscillator and 127 the servo rate — enough for eight expanders. Two boards for the dome and one for the body is comfortably inside it.

Old and new firmware are indistinguishable on the wire

An older bridge reads the channel out of six bits instead of seven. Send channel 70 to one and it decodes 70 & 0x3F = 6 and moves a completely different servo — no error, no clue. So the app reads the width off the banner at connect (PCA-BRIDGE 2+ or MAESTRO-PCA 3+ is wide; anything unrecognised is treated as narrow) and drops what the connected board cannot decode, with one plain warning, rather than folding it onto the wrong servo. Nothing needs re-flashing to keep working; re-flashing is what unlocks channels 32 and up.

16 · A real Maestro over USB

If your droid uses a Pololu Maestro, the simulator can talk to it directly. Plug it in, press Connect hardware, and the bench drives real servos — and, uniquely, reads back where they actually are.

  1. Put the Maestro in USB Dual Port mode (Pololu's Control Center, Serial Settings) — or any mode where the command port is not ignored.
  2. Close Control Center. One program per COM port; it will not work with both open.
  3. Pick the command port of the pair the board presents, not the TTL one.
  4. Connect. If nothing identifies itself, press it is a Pololu Maestro.
The port drives. It cannot configure.

This is a hard limit of the Maestro, not of this app. Min, max, neutral, home, channel mode and serial mode live behind the board's native USB interface, which a COM port cannot reach. Control Center writes those, once. There is no serial command for it — do not go looking.

The Finish step on a Maestro build says exactly what Control Center is still for, and hands you the settings .mstr that closes the gap.

The silent clamp — and why the read-back exists

A target outside a channel's stored limits is clamped by the board with no error and no reply. Ask 1000 µs of a channel whose stored minimum is 1136 and the servo stops at 1136: the dial keeps turning, the panel stops moving, and it reads exactly like a binding linkage or a dying servo. Control Center does not tell you either — it simply will not let you ask.

So the simulator asks the board where the servo actually is, several times a second, on whichever channel is under the dial. A servo that has settled somewhere other than where it was asked is named in microseconds, with the stored limit identified as the cause. If you see that message, the fix is in Control Center, not on your linkage.

Speed and acceleration, on the board vs in the app

A Maestro shapes every move itself, and this app has already shaped it. Both at once is slower than either, and it reads as "the model and the droid disagree". By default the board's stored speed and acceleration are left alone. One opt-in button zeroes them for the session so the engine alone shapes the move — a runtime change only, undone by a power cycle, and it says so.
